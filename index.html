How your heart's electrical system works

Your heart has four chambers. The two upper chambers are the atria, which collect blood and give it a gentle push into the lower chambers. The two lower chambers are the ventricles, which do the heavy work of pumping blood to the lungs and the rest of the body. For all of this to work, the chambers need to squeeze in the right order, and that order is set by the heart's electrical system.

A normal heartbeat starts in the sinus node, a small cluster of cells in the top of the right atrium. The sinus node is the heart's natural pacemaker. It sends out a signal that spreads across both atria and makes them squeeze. The signal then arrives at the AV node, which sits at the junction between the upper and lower chambers. The AV node is the only electrical doorway between the atria and the ventricles. It holds the signal for a fraction of a second so the ventricles have time to fill with blood. From there the signal travels down the bundle of His and splits into the right and left bundle branches. These are fast wires that carry the signal to every part of the ventricles almost at once, so both ventricles squeeze together in one strong, coordinated beat.

An EKG records this electrical activity from the skin. The small P wave is the atria being activated. The tall, sharp QRS complex is the ventricles being activated. The rounded T wave is the ventricles resetting for the next beat. When the sinus node is in charge and the signal follows the normal wiring, the tracing is called normal sinus rhythm.

The key idea behind every abnormal rhythm: the heart is full of cells that can generate electrical signals. In a normal heart the sinus node fires first and keeps everyone else in line. An arrhythmia happens when some other group of cells starts sending out its own rogue signals, or when a signal gets caught in a loop and keeps going around, so that the sinus node is no longer in charge. The rhythms

Normal sinus rhythm Normal

This is the normal rhythm of a healthy heart. The sinus node fires between 60 and 100 times per minute at rest, each signal follows the normal wiring, and the atria and ventricles squeeze in the proper order. Your heart rate rises with exercise, stress, fever, pain, or dehydration, and it slows during sleep or in people who are very fit. A fast sinus rhythm is called sinus tachycardia and a slow one is called sinus bradycardia. Both are usually the heart responding correctly to what the body needs rather than an electrical problem in the heart itself.

On the EKG

Every beat shows a P wave, then a narrow QRS complex, then a T wave, all evenly spaced.

Atrial fibrillation (AFib) Atria

What is happening

In atrial fibrillation, many different spots in the atria fire off electrical signals at the same time in a chaotic way. Most of these rogue signals come from the area where the pulmonary veins enter the left atrium. With hundreds of signals per minute coming from everywhere, the atria cannot squeeze properly. Instead they quiver. The AV node is bombarded with signals and lets some of them through at random, so the ventricles beat irregularly and often too fast.

What you might feel

Some people feel a fluttering, pounding, or irregular heartbeat. Others feel tired, short of breath, lightheaded, or notice that they cannot exercise like they used to. Many people feel nothing at all, and AFib is found on a routine EKG or a smartwatch.

Risk factors and causes

AFib becomes more common with age. Other risk factors include high blood pressure, heart failure, heart valve disease, coronary artery disease, obesity, diabetes, sleep apnea, an overactive thyroid, lung disease, and heavy alcohol use, including binge drinking. It can also be triggered temporarily by surgery, infection, or a serious illness. AFib sometimes runs in families.

How it is treated

Treatment has three main goals.

1. Preventing stroke. This is the most important goal. Because the atria quiver instead of squeezing, blood can pool and form clots, especially in a small pouch of the left atrium called the left atrial appendage. If a clot breaks loose it can travel to the brain and cause a stroke. Your doctor estimates your stroke risk using a score based on your age, sex, and medical conditions. Most people with AFib and other risk factors are advised to take a blood thinner (an anticoagulant) such as apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, or warfarin. People who cannot take blood thinners may be candidates for a procedure that seals off the left atrial appendage.

2. Rate control. Medicines such as beta blockers (metoprolol, carvedilol), calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil), or digoxin slow the AV node so that fewer signals get through to the ventricles. The AFib is still there, but the heart beats at a comfortable rate. For many people this is all that is needed.

3. Rhythm control. This means trying to restore and maintain normal sinus rhythm. Options include an electrical cardioversion (a brief, controlled shock delivered under sedation, often called a DCCV), antiarrhythmic medicines such as flecainide, propafenone, sotalol, dofetilide, or amiodarone, and catheter ablation, a procedure in which a cardiologist uses a thin tube to isolate the pulmonary veins so the rogue signals cannot reach the rest of the atrium. Rhythm control is often chosen for people who have troublesome symptoms, who are younger, or whose AFib is recent.

Blood thinners around a cardioversion. Restoring normal rhythm, whether by shock, by medicine, or on its own, can dislodge a clot that has been sitting quietly in the atrium. For this reason a cardioversion is normally done only after at least three weeks of uninterrupted blood thinner, or after an ultrasound from inside the esophagus confirms that there is no clot. Blood thinners are then continued for at least four weeks afterward, and usually long term if your stroke risk is high. Do not skip doses in the weeks around a cardioversion.

Treating the causes matters too. Controlling blood pressure, treating sleep apnea, losing weight, limiting alcohol, and staying active all reduce how often AFib comes back.

When to seek care right away

Go to the emergency department or call 911 for chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or any sign of stroke such as sudden weakness, facial droop, or trouble speaking.

On the EKG

There are no P waves. The baseline looks wavy or scribbly, and the QRS complexes are irregularly spaced with no pattern.

Atrial flutter Atria

What is happening

Atrial flutter is a close cousin of atrial fibrillation, but it is more organized. Instead of chaos, a single electrical signal gets caught in a loop that circles around the right atrium, usually around the tricuspid valve, about 300 times per minute. The AV node cannot pass signals that quickly, so it usually lets through every second one, giving a ventricular rate of about 150 beats per minute. Sometimes it lets through every third or fourth signal instead.

What you might feel

People often notice a fast, steady pounding heartbeat, tiredness, shortness of breath, or lightheadedness. Because the rate is often stuck at exactly 150, it can feel constant rather than fluttering.

Risk factors and causes

The risk factors are the same as for atrial fibrillation: age, high blood pressure, heart failure, valve disease, lung disease, sleep apnea, thyroid problems, alcohol, and recent heart surgery. Many people with flutter also have episodes of AFib, and the two often occur together.

How it is treated

Flutter carries the same risk of stroke as AFib, so the same blood thinner rules apply, including the rules around cardioversion. Rate control medicines can be used but often work less well than in AFib because the loop is so regular. Electrical cardioversion usually stops flutter easily. The best long-term treatment for typical flutter is catheter ablation, in which a small line of tissue in the right atrium is cauterized to break the loop. This cures typical flutter in more than 90 percent of people, which is why ablation is often recommended early.

On the EKG

Instead of P waves there is a continuous sawtooth pattern, with a QRS complex after every second or third tooth.

Ectopic atrial tachycardia Atria

What is happening

In ectopic atrial tachycardia, a single small cluster of cells somewhere in the atria, outside the sinus node, starts firing faster than the sinus node. Because it is faster, it takes over. Each of its signals spreads across the atria, passes through the AV node normally, and reaches the ventricles, so the heart beats fast but in an organized way, usually between 100 and 250 beats per minute. It can come in short bursts or run continuously for hours or days.

What you might feel

A racing or pounding heartbeat, sometimes with shortness of breath, lightheadedness, or chest discomfort. When it runs continuously for weeks or months without being noticed, the fast rate can weaken the heart muscle. This weakness usually recovers once the rhythm is controlled.

Risk factors and causes

Often no cause is found. It can be associated with underlying heart or lung disease, an overactive thyroid, low potassium or magnesium, too much caffeine, alcohol, or stimulants, and certain medicines. It is one of the rhythms that can be caused by too much digoxin.

How it is treated

Correcting triggers such as electrolytes, thyroid problems, and stimulants comes first. Beta blockers or calcium channel blockers can slow the rhythm or reduce how often it happens. Antiarrhythmic medicines are an option. Catheter ablation, in which the cardiologist finds the rogue cluster of cells and cauterizes it, can be a cure. This rhythm does not carry the same clot and stroke risk as atrial fibrillation because the atria still squeeze.

On the EKG

The rhythm is fast and regular. There is a P wave before every QRS, but it has a different shape from a normal sinus P wave because the signal starts in a different place.

AVRT (extra pathway, includes WPW) Loop

What is happening

AVRT stands for atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia. Some people are born with an extra strand of muscle that connects an atrium directly to a ventricle, bypassing the AV node. This is called an accessory pathway. Normally the insulating layer between the upper and lower chambers has only one doorway, the AV node. With an accessory pathway there are two. A signal can travel down through the AV node to the ventricles and then race back up the accessory pathway into the atria, where it starts the whole loop again. The loop spins round and round, driving the heart at 150 to 250 beats per minute. When the accessory pathway is visible on a resting EKG, the condition is called Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, or WPW.

What you might feel

Episodes typically start and stop suddenly, like a switch. People describe a racing heart, pounding in the neck, lightheadedness, shortness of breath, or anxiety. Fainting is uncommon but can occur.

Risk factors and causes

The accessory pathway is present from birth, so this rhythm is often first noticed in children, teenagers, or young adults. It can run in families. Episodes may be triggered by exercise, stress, caffeine, alcohol, or lack of sleep, but often there is no trigger at all.

How it is treated

During an episode, vagal maneuvers such as bearing down hard, or bearing down and then lying flat with the legs raised, can break the loop. In the emergency department a medicine called adenosine briefly stops the AV node and interrupts the loop within seconds. Longer term, catheter ablation of the accessory pathway is the preferred treatment. It cures the problem in about 95 percent of people and is usually recommended for anyone who has had symptoms. Beta blockers or calcium channel blockers can be used while waiting for ablation.

People with WPW who also develop atrial fibrillation can have dangerously fast heart rates, because the accessory pathway does not slow signals down the way the AV node does. For this reason some common heart medicines, including digoxin, verapamil, and diltiazem, are avoided in people known to have WPW. Make sure every doctor treating you knows you have an accessory pathway.

On the EKG

During an episode the rhythm is fast, regular, and narrow, with a small inverted P wave hiding just after each QRS. Between episodes a person with WPW may show a short PR interval and a slurred upstroke called a delta wave.

AVNRT (loop inside the AV node) Loop

What is happening

AVNRT stands for atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia. It is the most common cause of sudden fast heartbeat in adults with otherwise healthy hearts. In some people the AV node has two lanes rather than one: a fast lane and a slow lane. Usually this causes no problem. Occasionally, often after an early extra beat, a signal goes down the slow lane and comes back up the fast lane, and then repeats. The signal is now circling inside the AV node itself. With every lap it sends a signal down to the ventricles and up to the atria at the same time, driving the heart at 150 to 220 beats per minute.

What you might feel

A sudden racing heartbeat that starts and stops abruptly. Because the atria and ventricles squeeze at the same moment, many people feel a pounding or fluttering in the neck. Lightheadedness, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and a strong urge to urinate afterward are common. Fainting is rare.

Risk factors and causes

The two lanes are something you are born with. AVNRT is more common in women and often first appears in the teens through the forties. Episodes can be brought on by caffeine, alcohol, stress, exhaustion, stimulant medicines, or nothing in particular. In a structurally normal heart it is uncomfortable but not dangerous.

How it is treated

Vagal maneuvers, such as bearing down hard or the modified maneuver of bearing down and then lying back with the legs lifted, stop many episodes at home. In the emergency department, adenosine ends the rhythm within seconds in most cases, and diltiazem or a beta blocker are alternatives. For people with frequent episodes, daily beta blockers or calcium channel blockers reduce recurrences, and some people carry a single dose to take when an episode starts. Catheter ablation of the slow lane cures AVNRT in more than 95 percent of people, with a small risk of about 1 percent of damaging the AV node enough to need a pacemaker.

On the EKG

A fast, very regular, narrow rhythm. P waves are usually invisible because they are buried inside the QRS complexes.

Ventricular tachycardia (VT) Ventricles

What is happening

In ventricular tachycardia, the rogue signals come from the ventricles themselves rather than from the atria or the AV node. A patch of ventricular muscle, very often an area of scar from an old heart attack, begins firing on its own or traps a signal in a loop. Because the signal starts in the muscle and spreads slowly from cell to cell instead of using the fast wiring, the ventricles squeeze in an uncoordinated way at 120 to 250 beats per minute. The atria are no longer in charge and often keep beating on their own schedule in the background. At these speeds the ventricles do not have time to fill, so much less blood is pumped with each beat.

What you might feel

Palpitations, lightheadedness, chest pain, shortness of breath, or fainting. Sustained VT is a medical emergency because it can cause the blood pressure to collapse and can deteriorate into ventricular fibrillation, which is cardiac arrest. Short bursts of a few beats may cause no symptoms at all.

Risk factors and causes

The most common cause is scar tissue from a previous heart attack. Other causes include heart failure and a weakened heart muscle from any cause, inherited heart conditions such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, very low or high potassium or magnesium, certain medicines, and stimulant drugs such as cocaine. Some forms of VT occur in completely normal hearts and are much less dangerous.

How it is treated

If the blood pressure is low or the person is unwell, VT is treated immediately with an electrical shock (cardioversion). If the person is stable, medicines such as amiodarone, procainamide, or lidocaine may be tried. Fixing the cause matters: opening blocked arteries, correcting potassium and magnesium, and stopping offending drugs. To protect against sudden death, many people with VT and a weakened heart are offered an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD), a small device under the skin that watches the rhythm and delivers a shock if VT or ventricular fibrillation occurs. Beta blockers and antiarrhythmic medicines reduce how often VT happens, and catheter ablation can target the scar tissue where the loop lives.

Anyone who has fainted, or who has a fast heartbeat with chest pain, shortness of breath, or confusion, should call 911. Do not drive yourself.

On the EKG

The rhythm is fast and the QRS complexes are wide and bizarre looking, because the signal is spreading slowly through muscle instead of along the fast wiring.

Bundle branch blocks

A bundle branch block is not an arrhythmia. The sinus node is still in charge and the heart rate is normal. The problem is in the wiring below the AV node. One of the two bundle branches is blocked or slowed, so the signal reaches one ventricle late by spreading through the muscle from the other side. Both ventricles still squeeze, but not quite together. Bundle branch block is usually found by chance on an EKG, and the words "right bundle branch block" or "left bundle branch block" on an EKG report are what prompt many questions.

Right bundle branch block (RBBB) Wiring

What is happening

The right bundle branch is blocked. The signal travels normally down the left bundle branch, activates the left ventricle on time, and then spreads slowly across the septum through muscle to activate the right ventricle late.

What it means

Right bundle branch block is common and is often found in people with completely normal hearts, especially as they get older. It can also be seen with lung disease, a blood clot in the lungs, some congenital heart conditions, or after heart surgery. In the absence of other problems it does not cause symptoms, does not affect how well the heart pumps, and does not need treatment. Your doctor may order an echocardiogram (an ultrasound of the heart) once to make sure the heart itself is normal.

On the EKG

The rhythm and rate are normal, but each QRS complex is wide with a characteristic notched or slurred shape.

Left bundle branch block (LBBB) Wiring

What is happening

The left bundle branch is blocked. The signal travels down the right bundle branch, activates the right ventricle first, and then spreads slowly across the septum through muscle to activate the left ventricle late. Because the left ventricle is the main pumping chamber, this delay can make its squeeze less efficient.

What it means

Left bundle branch block is more likely than right bundle branch block to be a sign of an underlying heart condition, such as long-standing high blood pressure, coronary artery disease, a weakened heart muscle, or aortic valve disease. It can also appear after procedures on the aortic valve. Many people with LBBB feel entirely well. Your doctor will usually order an echocardiogram and sometimes a stress test to look for a cause. A new LBBB in someone with chest pain is treated as a possible heart attack until proven otherwise.

How it is treated

There is no treatment for the block itself, and it does not need one if the heart is otherwise healthy. If LBBB occurs together with heart failure and a weak heart muscle, a special pacemaker that resynchronizes the two ventricles (called CRT, or cardiac resynchronization therapy) can improve symptoms and heart function. Very rarely, bundle branch block progresses to a complete block of the AV node, which would require a regular pacemaker.

On the EKG

The rhythm and rate are normal, but each QRS complex is wide and often notched, and the T wave points the opposite way from the QRS.
